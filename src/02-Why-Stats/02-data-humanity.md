## 1. Question about "Seven intersectional feminist principles ..."

### 1.1 Data and power

**Question**: According to D'Ignazio and Klein, what principles concern data and "power"? Define and discuss each principle below. Be sure to note the important questions to ask about data and power.

**Answer**:

1) Examine Power: Identify the people/groups who benefit from unearned advantages, who make decisions that affect us all, who control the flow of information. 

2) Challenge Power: Attempt to right and rebalance the distribution of power (e.g., by collecting counterdata).

3) Elevate Emotion and Embodiment: Challenge the prevailing idea that data communication should always be neutral, “objective,” rational (because data concerns real people).

4) Rethink Binaries and Hierarchies: False binaries influence how data is used and collected, perpetuating hierarchy; thinking outside of a binary results in more comprehensive and inclusive data collection.

5) Embrace Pluralism: Strive to consider as many (diverse) perspectives as possible, particularly those most affected by the subject at hand.

6) Consider Context: Factor the impact of context (socio-political conditions/circumstances) into data interpretation.

7) Make Labor Visible: Good data is invaluable and necessary to our survival and livelihood but is often taken for granted. It’s important to remember that data work (and work in general) is done by people.

### 1.2 Data: Objective mirror, or not?

**Question**: What do D'Ignazio and Klein mean by issues of "false binaries and hidden hierarchies" (p. 3)? Then, explain how principles 3, 5, 6 and 7 each approach such issues.

**Answer**: The authors are referring to data that is missed or ignored when false binaries permeate data collection, and to how that absence of data enforces existing power structures. Principles 3 and 4 confront this issue by encouraging us to consider and prioritize the human side of data (i.e., data should help, not hurt, people, particularly our most vulnerable). Principle 6 asks us to acknowledge context, which approaches the issue by reminding us that data is subjective and can be used to push harmful agendas. Principle 7 confronts the issue by recognizing that work is done by people, the same people that hidden hierarchies seek to dismiss/devalue.

## 2. Questions about Queer Data

### 2.1 Defining "queer data" & "intersectional LGBTQ data"

**Question**: Create a bulleted list of short excerpts and summary statements where Guyan defines "queer data" and "intersectional LGBTQ data" in the introduction. Be sure to accompany all excerpts and paraphrases with page number locations in the PDF.

**Answer**:
- “Queer data is more than a study of individuals that sit outside the categories of heterosexual or cisgender. It is equally a brash, confrontational and in-your-face challenge to conventional understandings of how data and identities intersect…asks us to reconsider the notion that 'numbers speak for themselves'.” (p. 12)

- “Queer data is more than using data to tell stories about the lives and experiences of LGBTQ individuals: the presentation of data is also an opportunity for LGBTQ people to see themselves reflected, although this mirror image is never a truly accurate representation.” (p. 19)

- “Queer data brings together two distinct, though related, strands. Firstly, gender, sex and sexuality data particularly (though not exclusively) as it relates to the lives and experiences of individuals who identify as LGBTQ…the second strand of queer data, which examines the queering of research methods.” (p. 19, 21)

- “Queer data emerges as the product of tension between categories and anti-categories, assimilation and difference, intrinsic qualities and social constructs, and individuals and populations.” (p. 24)

- “Intersectional LGBTQ data tells a nuanced story that better reflects the messy and overlapping experiences of people's everyday lives.” (p. 29)

**Answer**:
Queer data: “The numbers *don’t* speak for themselves.”

### 2.2 Guyan's exigence for the book

**Question**: Why did Guyan write this book? What was his exigence?

**Answer**: Guyan wrote this book in order to not only provide the general public with more (nuanced) data on LGBTQ people in Scotland and the UK, but to interrogate the ways in which data on LGBTQ people is collected, interpreted, and used.

"I started to write this book in response to misconceptions I came across related to data about LGBTQ staff and students…It is a means to fight back against opponents working to silence and erase particular lives" (p. 16).


### 2.3 "Prescriptive" versus "descriptive"

**Question**: What does Guyan mean when he labels terms like gender, sex, and sexuality as being descriptive—*not* prescriptive (see pp. 13-14 in particular)? Explain the differences between the two with an example from each of the terms.

**Answer**: I think Guyan means that, in the context of his book, these terms represent tools people use to self-define. Prescriptive language imposes rigid standards while descriptive language allows for clear, thoughtful communication.

- Gender
  - Prescriptive: Teacher intentionally misgenders student.
  - Descriptive: Student expresses their preferred pronouns.

- Sex
  - Prescriptive: Individual who has transitioned is prevented from updating their gender on identifying documents.
  - Descriptive: Individual who has transitioned expresses that their biological sex does not align with gender.

- Sexuality
  - Prescriptive: Bisexual person in a heterosexual marriage is called “straight.”
  - Descriptive: Bisexual person in a heterosexual marriage identifies as bisexual.


### 2.4 "'Visibility is a trap'"

**Question**: Guyan cites Foucault's idea about how "'visibility is a trap'" (p. 20 of the PDF). What does this expression mean, and why does Guyan draw parallels between Foucault's original use of it with issues of power and data? Be sure to illuminate your discussion with specific examples that Guyan uses in the book.

**Answer**: The expression refers to how prisoners in a panopticon behave as if they are always being watched (that is, increased surveillance does not work in their favor and instead gives the prison guard more power). By invoking the expression, Guyan means to illustrate how increased visibility can result in increased exposure to danger. Lack of awareness of a group makes the people of that group less likely to be targeted.

Guyan proves this theory by pointing to how the number of “hate crimes where sexual orientation or transgender identity was an aggravating factor” have only increased in Scotland as more data on LGBTQ Scottish folks has been collected and made available.


## 3. Questions about "The topology of race"

**Context**: In this reading, you read a shorter excerpt from a longer review essay by Lee. In this review essay, Lee summarizes Kwame Anthony Appiah's collection of essays, *In My Father's House*, as a means of instigating new lines of inquiry for legal studies.

### 3.1 Race & racism: From conjecture to questions of definition

**Question**: Why does Lee think it important that Appiah changed the standard line of inquiry in legal studies from "whether racism exists" (p. 441) to "what race and racism are"? What "overdetermined" (p. 441) parts or race, i.e., issues about race, does this definitional question bring into focus for deliberation? Be sure to ***define*** and discuss Appiah's position about race as being essential/biological or not and being cultural or not. Indeed, pay close attention to Appiah's definition of culture.

**Answer**: Lee thinks it’s important because, by pivoting to this new line of inquiry, Appiah was able to open up the dialogue on race further and explore different dimensions of the subject (“By circumventing the typical questions we ask about race, Appiah opens up the impasses that currently constrain the dialogue” [p. 441]).

The issues about race brought to focus by this question are the widespread assumptions that 1) race is a set of biological qualities, and 2) race is fixed and limited. Appiah argues that race is not essential/biological, and that what is commonly seen as race is actually ethnic/cultural identity. He thinks that the “'subtle differences' of temperament, belief, and intention” among people are wrapped up in culture, or “[w]hat exists 'out there' in the world-communities of meaning, shading variously into each other in the rich structure of the social world....” (p. 442).


### 3.2 Race as multiplicitous

**Question**: What does Lee mean by "race is always multiplicitous because social contexts are multiplicitous" (p. 447)? Use the Shaw v. Reno case to help you explain how this challenges both essentialist/biological definitions of race & Appiah's race as synonomous with culture.

**Answer**: Lee means that race is more than one thing. Race is complex, and it has different meanings in different contexts (and can even have multiple meanings in a single context), and those contexts often overlap and cannot be regarded independently.

The outcome of the Shaw v. Reno case was informed by the assumption that race is based on skin color alone (i.e., to think that all people of the same skin color are the same is racist, so any acknowledgment of skin color is racist), a biological definition of race. The case outcome was unsatisfactory because of its reliance on this inadequate definition of race, one that “does not recognize the nonbiological dimensions of race” and rejects “the possibility of a nonstigmatic use of ‘race’” (p. 446).

Appiah's theory of race being nothing more than cultural identity is also problematic because it ignores how “traditional notions of race have shaped, and continue to shape, the societies in which we live” and “may actually hinder our ability to resist entrenched forms of racism” (p. 442).

## 4. Questions about a data team's "rhetorical process of data"

### 4.1 What's the "god trick" problem?

**Question**: Briefly describe the "god trick" problem. Also, discuss what connections you recognize between the "god trick" problem and some issues raised in our other readings.

**Answer**: The “god trick” problem refers to the belief that science data work is objective and are not impacted by the various contexts of those involved in it. This directly relates to the principles explored in the D'Ignazio and Klein reading (particularly “Elevate Emotion and Embodiment” and “Consider Context”) as well as the positivist vs. structural perspective discussion in the first chapter of *Statistic for Social Justice*.

### 4.2 What's stasis and stasis networks?

**Question**: Briefly define stasis and stasis networks. Explain what stasis networks provides qualitative researchers?

**Answer**: Stasis is a procedure that generates an agreed-upon result on an issue by applying a number of “guiding questions called stases: questions of fact, definition, cause, quality, and action” (p. 446-447). Stasis network methodology is a tool that enables data workers to identify and understand the rhetorical actions they make when they report data; it makes “the ephemeral aspects of data work more legible” (p. 476), which I gather allows for increased awareness of previously unaccounted-for influences on the data professionals' work.

### 4.3 What's the "DMT cluster"?

**Question**: What's the *DMT cluster*, and how did connect to observations about the data team's judgment about the *quality of metrics*?

**Answer**: The “DMT cluster” refers to definitive, metrics, and translative nodes, which were found to be the most frequent co-occurrences across the MAPS projects detailed in the text. This information led the team to significantly reevaluate their work, as their metrics were suddenly inadequate: “DMT work in relationship to alignment was a hotspot of negotiation to expose the constraints and limitations of data. Within these moments, their deliberations to define metrics involved contextualizing and (re)defining metrics by critically comparing and contrasting external provenances against their current reporting goals” (p. 491).
